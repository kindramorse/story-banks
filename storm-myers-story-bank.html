01If I miss this workout, I'll go back to being fatREADY · YOUR LANE

The belief that ran your mornings, and the Saturday you finally proved it wrong. This is the clearest, most on-brand story you own. It IS your offer, shown instead of explained.

His words, from the session: "If my consistency fails, then I fail, then I go back to being fat." / "If I miss this workout on Saturday, I'll go back to being fat."

Story post · single image or b-roll, story in caption

Depth build. Open in the scene, dwell on the cost, land on the strawberry patch. Aimed at the dad who lives in all-or-nothing.

Hook options
  • For years, a canceled workout could ruin my whole day. context
  • My wife would suggest a trip and my stomach would drop. intrigue
  • I thought skipping one workout meant I'd lose everything. contrarian
For a long time, a birthday party could wreck me. My wife would say it so gently. "So-and-so's party is Saturday morning." And my whole body would go tight. Jaw. Fists. My brain already spinning. Because Saturday morning was my workout. And if I couldn't get the workout in, the day was already lost. So I'd start negotiating. Can I walk there? What if it rains? Maybe we go Saturday instead of Friday. Maybe I get up before anyone. I'd spend the whole week solving a math problem nobody else knew I was doing. Underneath all of it was one sentence I never said out loud. If I miss this workout, I'll go back to being fat. That was the belief. Miss one Saturday, lose everything. I can't tell you how many Friday nights and Saturday mornings my family spent around a version of me that was there, but not really there. Then a few weeks ago, my wife said she wanted to take the kids to a strawberry patch. And for the first time, I didn't do the math. We slept in. We cuddled. We got out the door and stood in line in the sun. I didn't get a workout in that day. I didn't go back to being fat. I was just there. All the way there, for the whole thing.
CTA · three clean beatsAll-or-nothing thinking will cost you years of Saturdays before it ever costs you a pound. If you're a dad who can't enjoy the day until the workout's done, I see you. Follow along, I'm putting words to this all week.

This is your strongest opener because it's clean, relatable, and it demonstrates your whole offer without a single 'here's what I help with' line. The landing ('I was just there') is a held beat, do not add a lesson after it.

03The son who only wanted his momPERSONAL · YOUR CALL

The rejection you felt when your second son didn't want you, the guilt you carried for months, and the night you decided to give your wife her sleep back. Powerful and deeply personal.

This one is tender. You shared it while working something out in real time, not as content. It's yours. It could help a lot of dads who feel this and never say it, but only post it if and when you're ready, and only in a way that feels safe for you and your marriage. There's no version of this worth publishing that you'd regret.

His words, from the session: "He would just blood-curdle scream if I tried to hold him... it's not even me doing something wrong. He literally just does not want me."

Story post · single image or b-roll, story in caption

Depth. Open in the scene, dwell on the cost (the rejection and the guilt), land on the repair. Villain is the belief, never your wife or your son.

Hook options
  • No one warns you that your own baby might not want you. intrigue
  • My first son bonded with me instantly. My second wouldn't let me hold him. contrarian
  • For months I believed my son not wanting me was my punishment. context
With my first son, it was instant. He came out and within minutes I thought, I love you, I've got you. My second son didn't want me. He'd blood-curdle scream if I tried to hold him. Only mom. Not at bedtime, not at 1:30 in the morning, not at 3:30. Just her. And I'm good with kids. That's the part that got me. It wasn't that I was doing something wrong. He just did not want me. So I told myself a story about it. That I'd earned this. I'd left for a wedding right after he was born. I'd been sick and useless his first day. Somewhere in there I decided his rejection was a punishment I just had to endure. So at night, when I heard him before she did, I'd stay still. Tell myself she'd handle it. Tell myself I needed the sleep to be a good dad tomorrow. The truth was quieter than that. If I got up, he'd scream, and I'd feel it all over again. So I stayed in bed to avoid being rejected by my own son. Months of that. Then one night, both of us wrecked, him crying, neither of us sleeping, I heard myself think: this is not what I want our life to be. So I stopped waiting to be wanted. I started getting up. Taking the nights I could take, so she could finally sleep. He's eleven months old now. He knows my voice in the dark.
CTA · three clean beatsThe story you tell yourself about why you're on the outside is usually the thing keeping you there. If you're a dad who's felt like the backup parent in your own house, you're not the only one. Follow along.

If you ever post this, land it on the repair ('he knows my voice in the dark'), never on the shame. The guilt is the middle of the story, not the end. Keep your wife blameless in the telling, the villain is the belief that his rejection was your punishment.
