Signature Story Bank

Storm Myers — the stories that reveal who you are

Pulled from our story excavation session. Every story below is a real moment from your life, dug out and shaped so it does the work for you: it shows the reader who you are instead of telling them.

You help dads who are on a health journey and stuck in all-or-nothing thinking. Not "all dads." Him. The guy who's so locked in on the plan he misses the moment. You’re talking to an earlier version of yourself, and that’s exactly why the stories land.
The Throughline Method: we did The Dig together. This is The Reveal. One law runs under all of it: character can’t be claimed, only revealed.

How to use this bank

Each story is a section. Click the dropdowns under it to open the different posts you can build from that one moment. One story feeds a week of content.

The captions are written in your voice, to be dropped over simple B-roll (you doing something you’re already doing) or a static photo. That’s the format Kindra’s research keeps landing on: story in the caption, so the reader sets their own pace. No two-second scene changes, no heavy editing.

End every story on the moment. Don’t explain what it meant. The reader reaching the conclusion themselves is the entire point.

Story 01 · The core one
The Saturday Workout

The winter you’d rather leave Saturday morning than Friday night, because Friday night meant maybe missing your walk. And a missed walk meant, somewhere deep down, you were going back to being the old you.

The momentWinter 2025. A cousin’s birthday party comes up. Your brain starts spinning the schedule.
The forkLeave Friday with the family, or protect the ritual and go Saturday after the workout.
The costFriday nights and Saturday mornings your whole family missed. You, mean and tense, before anyone even packed a bag.
The villain (a force)All-or-nothing thinking. The belief that if it’s not exactly the plan, it doesn’t count.
Story post B-roll + caption ▶
Caption
"So-and-so’s birthday party is next weekend." That’s all my wife had to say. One sentence. And my brain was already gone. Okay. If we leave Friday night, I lose my Saturday walk. If it rains I can’t walk there. I need my barbell, my machines, my 1,200 calories, or it doesn’t count. So maybe we go Saturday morning. Maybe I get up at 5. Maybe I plan a date night so Friday at home is worth it. A birthday party. That’s what I was solving like a math problem. Here’s the sentence I never said out loud, the one running the whole thing: if I miss this workout, I go back to being fat. That’s it. That was the belief. And so I’d win the argument in my head, and we’d leave Saturday, and I’d get my walk in. And I’d have no idea how many Friday nights I just handed away. My kids, my wife, in the next room, while I sat there with my jaw locked and my fists tight, doing math.
Ends on the moment (him, jaw locked, doing math). No "and that’s why" line. Let it sit.
Soft CTA options (pick one, keep it light)

  • Comment "MATH" if your brain has ever done this to a birthday party invite.
Loop check — Stakes: a clock (the party is "next weekend") and a character at risk (the family time). Question: does he win the argument in his head, and what does it cost? Head fake: the villain isn’t his wife or the party, it’s the sentence he never said out loud. Rehook: the last line opens the real cost he couldn’t see.
Belief-shift post a stance, short ▶
Caption
"If I miss this workout, I’ll go back to being fat." I actually believed that. One skipped Saturday and the whole thing collapses. That’s not discipline. That’s all-or-nothing thinking wearing discipline’s jacket. One missed walk was never going to undo a year of work. But the belief that it would? That cost me Friday nights I’m never getting back. The workout was never the problem. The rule underneath it was.
Belief-shift / conviction. Runs on clarity, not arc. Good for B-roll of you walking, or a static shot.

Story 02 · The turn
The Strawberry Patch

The Saturday your wife suggested something fun, for the first time you can remember, and you said yes before your brain could run the numbers.

The momentA recent Saturday. "I think it’d be really fun to go to the strawberry patch." You slept in. You cuddled. You got out early to be first in line.
The forkGet up, work out, be back, "all good" — the old way. Or let the morning be the morning.
The head fakeYou didn’t work out that day. And your life didn’t end. You didn’t get fat.
The villain (a force)The same all-or-nothing pull, quieter now, asking if you’d even let yourself enjoy it.
Story post B-roll + caption ▶
Caption
My wife said, "I think it’d be really fun to go to the strawberry patch." I don’t think she’d ever said anything like that before. And I almost missed how big that was. Three months ago I know exactly how that Saturday goes. I’m up before anyone. Workout done, back home, "all good." We still go to the patch. But I’m there and I’m not there. Half my head is still on the calories I did or didn’t burn. This time we slept in. We cuddled. We got out the door early so we’d be first in line, baby on my chest, and that mattered to me more than anything I could’ve done at 6am. No workout that day. I ate the strawberries. And I was actually in it, the whole thing, start to finish.
Cut test passes: ends on "I was actually in it." No lesson bolted on. If you want a companion belief line, keep it separate, don’t weld it here.
Soft CTA options
  • Dads, when’s the last time you were all the way there for something small?
  • Comment "PRESENT" if you needed the reminder today.
Loop check — Stakes: her rare suggestion + the clock of the morning. Question: does he let himself enjoy it, or does the old pull win? Head fake: the win isn’t the trip, it’s that he skipped the workout and nothing fell apart. Rehook: "I was actually in it" pays off the whole open.
Belief-shift post priority flip ▶
Caption
I used to work out first, then try to enjoy my family with whatever was left. Now the joy comes first and the workout is the byproduct. Same guy. Same gym. Completely different life. It was never about doing less. It was about what I let sit at the top of the list.
Watch the "it’s not X it’s Y" cadence in that last line — it’s used once, on purpose. Don’t stack another one.

Story 03 · The one that costs something
1:30 In The Morning

The night you both hit the wall, and you gave up the one thing you’d protected the hardest so she could finally sleep.

The moment1:30am, a couple weeks ago. Baby crying. Both of you angry, both awake, both done. "This is dumb. I can’t do this anymore."
The forkKeep protecting your early mornings (your sacred workout window), or hand them over.
The costYou gave up waking up early. The thing you’d guarded above almost everything. So she could sleep.
The villain (a force)The belief that your needs had to come first for you to be okay. That giving meant losing.
Story post B-roll + caption ▶
Caption
1:30 in the morning. The baby’s crying. We’re both awake and we’re both mad and neither of us is moving. I remember thinking, this is dumb. This should not be happening. Somebody is lacking something here. For 11 months I’d protected my early mornings like they were sacred. Up before the house, workout in, that was mine. And my wife hadn’t slept a full night in almost a year. Her wrists hurt from holding him. Her back was shot. And I’d been telling myself, well, he only wants her anyway, so I’ll just get my hour of sleep and be the fun dad at 6:30. That night I did the math different. This is my problem to solve. What can I actually do. So I gave it up. The early mornings. I take him now. Before 10, he’s mine. Two nights this week she slept all the way through, for the first time in almost a year. I didn’t lose the mornings. I just finally spent them on the right thing.
This one earns its one line of "useful placard" at the end because it states a law the reader couldn’t reach on their own. Keep it. It’s the exception, not the rule.
Soft CTA options
  • Dads, what’s the thing you’re protecting that you might need to hand over?
  • Comment "TAG ME IN" if it’s time to stop keeping score at home.
Loop check — Stakes: a hard clock (1:30am) + real bodies at risk (her sleep, her wrists). Question: what does he do at the wall. Head fake: the sacrifice is the thing he’d protected the most. Rehook: the last line reframes giving as spending, not losing.
Belief-shift post serving vs. keeping score ▶
Caption
For a long time I gave to my family like I was going to want the change back. Do a little. Enough to feel like enough. Keep the rest for me, because if I don’t look out for me, who will. Then I started giving without waiting for the ring to come back. Turns out that’s where the peace was the whole time. Not in getting my hour. In closing the gap between the dad I looked like and the dad I actually am.
"Getting the ring back" is your phrase from the session. It’s yours, it’s specific, keep it.

Story 04 · Handle with care
The Son Who Only Wanted Mom

You’re great with kids. Every kid. Except the one who’d scream the second you picked him up. And the story you told yourself about why.

The momentYour second son, months old, blood-curdle screaming the second you tried to hold him. Only wanted mom.
The forkKeep trying and keep feeling the rejection, or decide "he just wants what he wants" and stop.
The costMonths of telling yourself it was a punishment you had to endure. Bonded in the day, never at night.
The villain (a force)Learned helplessness. The story that this is just how it is and there’s nothing you can do.
Story post B-roll + caption ▶
Caption
I’m good with kids. Any age, any kid, always have been. Except mine. My second son would scream the second I picked him up. Not a fussy cry. A full blood-curdle, put-me-down scream. And it wasn’t me doing anything wrong. He just did not want me. Only mom. So I told myself a story. This is a punishment. I wasn’t there enough early on, so this is what I get, and I just have to endure it. He wants what he wants and there’s nothing I can do. I carried that one for months. What I couldn’t see back then: when I’d hear him cry at 2am and not get up, I’d tell myself I needed the sleep. That she’d get him, they’d bond, everybody wins. The truth was quieter than that. I stayed in bed so I wouldn’t have to feel him reject me one more time.
Ends on the real reason, shown not named. Powerful and vulnerable. See flags before posting — this involves your marriage and your kids.
Soft CTA options
  • Dads, anyone else ever get quietly rejected by your own kid? What did you tell yourself?
  • Comment "SEEN" if you’ve stayed in bed to avoid a feeling before.
Loop check — Stakes: "except mine" lands in the second line. Question: why does the kid who’s great with everyone get rejected at home, and what does he do with it. Head fake: the reason he stayed in bed wasn’t sleep, it was fear of rejection. Rehook: final line reframes the whole thing.
Belief-shift post the hidden intention ▶
Caption
"I need the sleep." That’s what I told myself at 2am when my son was crying and I didn’t get up. It wasn’t about sleep. It was about not wanting to feel rejected one more time. We do this everywhere. "I’m not ready." "I don’t have my offer dialed." "I’ll start next week." It’s the same move. We’re not lazy or tired. We’re just protecting ourselves from a "no." Your brain will always dress up avoidance as a good reason. Learning to spot the costume is most of the work.
This bridges the parenting story to your dad-and-business audience without welding a sales pitch on. Good bridge, keep it soft.

  • My own son would scream the second I picked him up.
  • I told myself it was a punishment I had to endure. I carried that for months.
  • "I need the sleep" was never about sleep.
Story 05 · The shame one
The Burrito And The Wedding

You were in the best shape of your life the week your son was born. And that’s exactly what set up the worst version of you as a dad.

The momentHospital cafeteria, hours before your son arrives. Four months of restriction, then a giant burrito, tater tots, an energy drink you never drink.
The forkAll in on the plan, or all off the rails. There was never a middle.
The costViolently sick the first day of his life. Flat on the couch while your wife did it alone. Then a wedding, a week later, without her.
The villain (a force)All-or-nothing again, this time as the binge. Four months of "perfect" is what built the blowout.
Story post B-roll + caption ▶
Caption
I was in the best shape of my life the week my son was born. I want to tell you that like it’s a flex. It’s not. It’s the setup. We got to the hospital early. Went down to the cafeteria to kill time. And there’s this burrito the size of my forearm, tater tots, the works. I hadn’t eaten like that in four months. I told myself, half of this and some tots. I ate all of it. Chugged an energy drink I never drink. He was born the next day. And I was on the couch, grossly sick, useless, while my wife did the whole thing on her own. Four months of perfect. That’s what built the blowout. There was never a middle setting. It was all in or all off the rails, and my son’s first day got the off-the-rails version of me.
Strong, raw, and it names the mechanism (four months of perfect built the blowout) without a self-congratulating "lesson." See flags — this is heavy and personal.
Soft CTA options
  • Anyone else ever "earn" a blowout after weeks of white-knuckling it? That’s not willpower failing. That’s the setup.
  • Comment "MIDDLE" if you’ve only ever had two settings.
Loop check — Stakes: "best shape of my life the week my son was born" + the clock of the birth. Question: how does peak fitness become the worst day. Head fake: the fitness wasn’t the win, it was the fuse. Rehook: "there was never a middle" turns a story into a pattern the reader recognizes.
Belief-shift post the binge is the plan’s shadow ▶
Caption
Four months of perfect eating. One burrito the size of my arm, and I couldn’t stop. For years I thought the binge was me being weak. It wasn’t. It was the bill coming due for a plan that had no room in it. No flex, no bite, no birthday cake. The stricter the rules, the bigger the blowout waiting on the other side. That’s not a discipline problem. That’s all-or-nothing doing exactly what it does.
Belief-shift version, safer to lead with than the full birth story if you want to test the theme first.

Story 06 · The origin story
The Answering Machine

The day you heard your own voice played back and decided something was wrong with you. And how that one wound quietly built the thing you’re best at.

The momentTen, maybe twelve. The landline answering machine. "Hey mom, I’ll be home after practice." Then: oh my god, that’s what I sound like?
The forkA deep voice you got made fun of for. You decided it meant you were defective.
The costYou built a whole career (sign language, interpreting) partly so you could talk to anyone and they’d never have to hear your voice.
The gift underneathTo fit in, you got hyper-perceptive. You read the tiny changes other people miss. That’s the gift. This is what it cost.
Story post B-roll + caption ▶
Caption
I was maybe ten. Standing by the landline, listening to my own message play back. "Hey mom, dad, I’ll be home after practice." And I froze. Oh my god. That’s what I sound like? My voice came in deep and early, and kids are kids. Kermit growls. People doing the low voice back at me. I decided the simplest thing a kid can decide: something is wrong with me. I’m defective. So I found a language where I never had to use it. Sign language. I picked it up faster than anyone around me, and I couldn’t tell you why for years. I could see the tiniest change in someone’s hands, their face, a shift most people would blow right past. Here’s what I know now that I didn’t then. I got that good at watching people because I needed it. I needed to read the room before the room got to me. The thing I’m best at, seeing people, came from the years I didn’t feel seen myself.
This is your deepest story and your best "why I do what I do" piece. It earns one line of law at the end (the gift came from the wound). Keep it. Don’t add a second.
Soft CTA options
  • What’s the thing you’re best at that started as something you were trying to survive?
  • Comment "SEEN" if you know exactly what that feels like.
Loop check — Stakes: a kid + a moment of self-judgment + the clock of it happening in real time by the machine. Question: what does that decision cost him. Head fake: the deep voice he hated built his greatest strength. Rehook: "came from the years I didn’t feel seen myself" pays it all off.
Belief-shift post the gift came at a cost ▶
Caption
The thing you’re most generous with is usually the thing nobody gave you. I read people fast. Tiny shifts, the stuff most guys walk past. People think it’s a talent. It’s a scar. I learned to watch that closely because as a kid I needed to know which way a room was going to turn on me. Look at the dad who over-explains everything. He got left in the dark once. The guy who won’t let anyone feel stupid was made to feel stupid. The good stuff we do usually has a quiet wound sitting under it. Yours does too. It’s worth knowing where it came from.

Story 07 · Conviction / exploratory (read the flag)
Not My Language

You could make a fortune teaching sign language. You won’t. And the reason is a knot worth thinking out loud about, not preaching about.

The tensionYou know ASL inside out. People beg you to teach them. There’s real money in it.
The line you holdYou’re a hearing, non-native signer. Much of the Deaf community holds that teaching their language isn’t yours to do.
The open questionWhere’s the line between honoring a community and abandoning something you genuinely love and could help people with?
Format noteThis is unresolved for you. Post it as an honest, out-loud wrestle, not a stance. Curiosity, not a verdict.
Exploratory post honest, out-loud, no verdict ▶
Caption
I could probably make a lot of money teaching sign language. I know it inside out. People ask me all the time. And I’ve said no for years. Here’s the honest knot. I’m hearing. I’m not a native signer. And a big part of the Deaf community holds that their language isn’t mine to teach, that a hearing person teaching it chips away at something that belongs to them. I take that seriously. I’ve sat in that community, I love it, and I don’t want to take from it. But I’d be lying if I said there wasn’t another voice in me. The one that says there are people who genuinely want to learn, to talk to a deaf cousin, a coworker, a friend. And I love teaching it. I don’t have this one wrapped up. I’m saying it out loud because I think that’s more honest than pretending I do.
Do not turn this into a hot take. It’s sensitive and you’re still working it out. Ending on "I don’t have this wrapped up" is the whole point. Invite conversation, don’t declare a winner. See the flags section.

  • No easy answer here. Curious how you’d hold it.

  • No em dashes anywhere, per house style. Commas and periods do the work.
