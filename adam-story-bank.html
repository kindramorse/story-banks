Adam's Story Bank

Your core stories, each one built out into every format it can take. One story, many ways to tell it, so you never run out of content and never have to start from a blank page.

How to use this

Each panel below is one core story from your life and work. Open it and you'll find the story told once in full, then a set of formats: a carousel, a conviction post, a talking-head reel, a single static image, a b-roll caption. Same story, different shape, different job.

Pick the format that fits the week. Every panel is labeled with its post type, so you can see at a glance what job it does. They all pull from the same well.

This document grows. We add a new core story each time we find one, and you keep it after we're done working together. It's yours.

One quick convention. Any line shown in a framed box is text that goes ON the image or video. The grey box beneath it is the caption you paste into the post.

The four post types

A full brand runs on four kinds of post. Story is the thread underneath all of them, but each one does a different job. Every panel below is tagged with which it is.

Story post. Tells one real moment with an arc, and ends on the moment so you reach the point yourself. It never announces the lesson. Example: Why I Walked Away.

Belief-shift post. Plants a flag. A single stance he won't back off, no arc, revealing who he is through what he refuses to accept. Example: Why I Don't Take Every Case.

How-to post. Teaches the reader something they didn't know, following HEIT: hook, explain, illustrate, teach. The illustrate beat carries a real moment, not a generic tip, which is what keeps it his. Example: Why You Haven't Heard From Your Lawyer.

Invitation post. The clean ask. It states things plainly instead of withholding, and points to the next step. Example: If You're Not Sure You Even Have a Case.

01 Why I Walked Away
Story post · all formats ▶
The Story

He had the safe version of a legal career locked in: a government pension that would pay 100% of his salary, a track to retire in ten years, and a spot in line to become a city attorney, a role he'd even been offered at another city. One afternoon, walking out of the county building on the bay in San Diego, he saw a billionaire's yacht with a helicopter on the back and thought about the kid he used to be, the one who wanted to take on the world. He realized that if he stayed, he'd be comfortable for life but never get to do the one thing he actually wanted, stand next to one person and fight until they won. So he gave up the pension and the security and set out to build his own firm.

The trait it reveals: he plays to win, and playing to win for himself means playing to win for his clients. Villain: the safe path that asks you to trade your ambition for comfort, a force, never a person or firm.

02 The Silver Platter
Story post · all formats ▶
The Story

Early in his career, a newspaper wrote that the only reason he had his job as a rookie deputy city attorney was his last name, that he should go earn his spurs somewhere his father wasn't calling the shots. To take that job, he'd already given up his own small firm and the clients paying him. There were meetings among city officials about whether he should keep it at all. The man who recruited him said, "Well, I guess we'll find out if you still have a job." He figured he'd be gone within the week.

What no one saw: his dad coached him his whole childhood and expected more of him than anyone on the team. He could play the best game of his life on the basketball court and the whole drive home would be about the one shot he missed. His dad knew he was capable of straight A's, so anything less meant he hadn't given it everything. He didn't fully understand it as a kid, but the lesson underneath was that every shot counts, and winning most of the time isn't enough when you're capable of more. A win means little if you left one on the table you could have had. So when the paper said he hadn't earned his seat, he didn't argue. He refused to leave a case, a motion, or a negotiation with anything on the table. He won or settled every case he had there, negotiated the big deals, and argued an appellate motion most lawyers never get to touch. He won that too. The doubt never really flipped. He just out-worked it until there was nothing left to say.

The trait it reveals: he does his best work when he's underestimated, and he credits the father who raised him to earn every inch. Villain: public assumption, never a person.

06 Why You Haven't Heard From Your Lawyer
How-to post · teaches the reader ▶
What It Teaches

This one teaches the reader something no one tells them. A lot of firms would rather wait for a settlement than file a lawsuit and fight for more. Litigation is hard and slow. Waiting on a settlement is just as slow, and it pays you less. So the case sits, and you stop hearing from your lawyer.

The mechanism most people never hear: before a suit is filed, your case sits with a mid-level adjuster who runs your injury through a computer program that spits out a number, like an order screen at a drive-through. Nothing moves it. The day a suit is filed, it leaves that desk. A manager weighs in, the other side has to bring in a real attorney, and the number moves. He files fast, because that's the quickest way to get the most before evidence quietly disappears.

Structure (HEIT): hook names the problem, explain lays out the mechanism, illustrate carries a real case, teach hands over what to demand. The trait it reveals: he files and fights when a lot of firms would rather settle and move on. Villain: the reluctance to litigate that leaves money on the table, never a named firm. Adam has never run a practice this way; he just knows many do.

Format 1 · How-to Post (primary)

One static image, the caption runs the HEIT beats: hook, explain, illustrate a real case, teach.

If your lawyer hasn't called you in months, your case is probably sitting still.

If your lawyer hasn't called you in months, your case is probably sitting still. And a case nobody is pushing settles for less.

A lot of firms would rather wait for a settlement than file a lawsuit and fight for more. Litigation is hard and slow. Waiting on a settlement is just as slow, and it pays you less. So the case sits, and you stop hearing from anyone.

Here's what most people never get told. Before a lawsuit is filed, your case sits with a mid-level adjuster who runs your injury through a computer program, and it spits out a number. Like an order screen at a drive-through. The day a suit gets filed, your case leaves that desk. A manager has to weigh in, the other side has to bring in a real attorney, and the number moves.

I had one where the initial number came back estimated at $17,000. We filed, and about sixteen weeks later it settled for $200,000.

I file fast. It's the quickest way to get you the most, before evidence quietly disappears.

You don't have to hire me. But hire someone who files.

Format 1 · How-to Post (primary)

The first number they hand you is rarely the real one.

The first number an insurance company hands you is rarely the real one.

Most injury payouts are capped by the policy. What almost nobody checks is whether there's more than one. A second policy, an umbrella policy, coverage on another party who was also at fault. The adjuster quotes the first one and stops. So does a lot of the intake at a high-volume firm, because pulling every policy takes time no one there is paid to spend.

So before you sign anything, ask one question. Has every policy that could apply to my case been pulled and read. If the answer comes slow, that's your answer.
