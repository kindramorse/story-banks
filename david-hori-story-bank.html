Make it so

Post this first. Every other post in this bank is a room where somebody said no. This is the one where somebody said yes, and a reader has to feel that before any of the rest of it means anything.

Format + dialStory post. Run for depth.
B-rollA dish pit. Bus tubs stacked to the ceiling. Hands sorting silverware. Steam. A commercial kitchen at the tail of a rush. Nothing staged and nothing pretty.

Hooks text on screen

  • ContextMy first real job was the best run business I have ever worked for. It took me until my seventeenth job to know that.
  • ContrarianThe best leader I ever had was the one I almost never saw.
  • IntrigueWe redesigned the dish pit ourselves without being asked. Then we had to go tell the person in charge.

Caption

Hundreds of dishes came back into that kitchen at the same time. Every night, the same wave.

We were losing to it. The silverware would come out of the machine still dirty and go back through again, and we would still be standing there long after the floor was clean.

So we built something. One person on silverware and nothing else. Buckets of pre-soak, so it came out clean the first time instead of the third. A triage order for everything else.

None of that was our job. Nobody asked us for it.

I remember bringing it to her. I remember not being nervous, and that is the part it took me another sixteen jobs to understand.

She asked a couple of questions. Had we thought about the layout. Where the buckets would sit.

Then she said, great, do it. Make it so.

She was barely in the building. That was the most impressive thing about her, and I could not have told you that at the time. She hired the right people, gave them the systems and the standard, held everybody to it, and got out of the way.

It was impossible to be bad at your job there. The people who were bad at it felt it, and they left before anybody had to have a conversation with them.

I thought every job was going to be like that.

DIGThe year, the city, and what she was doing when you brought it to her. Standing at a pass, sitting in an office, halfway out the door with her keys. Right now the reader has your words and no room to put them in. And tell me how old you were, because I have written this as a young man and if you were thirty it is a different post.

A bomb in the kitchen

Post this second. Same kitchen as Post 1, and that is the whole engine. A reader who has just watched somebody say "make it so" is going to feel this one in their chest.

Format + dialStory post. Run hot for reach.
B-rollA commercial steamer. A pressure gauge. A hand hovering near a switch and not touching it. A wall phone. Steam venting. Nothing that identifies a real kitchen.

Hooks text on screen

  • ContextA machine in our kitchen started making a noise it had never made before. We told the owner. He told us to run it anyway.
  • ContrarianWe were told to operate a pressure cooker we thought was going to fail, or be fired. So we picked a third option.
  • IntrigueThere was a bomb in our kitchen and the only person who could do anything about it had already decided we were being dramatic.

Caption

There was a piece of machinery in that kitchen that started making noises it was not supposed to make.

It was a pressure cooker. If you have never worked a line, that is basically a bomb in the kitchen. That is not a dramatic way to put it, that is what it is.

We were nervous about running it. We said so.

We also brought a workaround. There was a way to run service that would have cut how much we needed that machine, and we brought it, because that is what we had been trained to do in that building. See a problem, bring a fix.

We were told we were running it anyway. That whether we felt safe was not the point. Run it or you are fired.

I could not fathom it. I had spent that whole job being taken seriously, and I stood there in an apron trying to work out how the thing every person in the room could hear was somehow not real.

So we called OSHA.

Nobody got shut down. What happened is that he found out he could not steamroll us, and a tech came out, and we worked around that machine until somebody finally looked at it.

He did not lose a single thing he could put a number on.

DIGWhere you were standing when he said run it or you are fired, and who else was in the kitchen. Then the phone call. Who made it, what was said, and what the room did in the ten seconds after. Right now the reader is told OSHA got called and never gets to watch anybody pick up a phone, and that phone call is the bravest thing in this bank.

Loop check

The cover carries a character, a risk and a clock, and the clock is a machine that is getting worse while people are arguing about it. One question: do you run it. The head fake is the third option, because a reader is holding two doors, comply or quit, and you walk through a door they did not know was there. The cover does not spend it.

Notes

  • The owner is not the villain. The villain is a business run on control, where the cheapest thing on the shelf is somebody else's nerve. He is never named and barely described, and he is the same man as Post 3, which is why these two run back to back.
  • This is the post that does the most work for the reader you want. A business owner reads "run it or you are fired" and does not think about you at all. They think about the last time somebody on their team said something was wrong and they decided it was drama. That is the entire job.
  • The last line is the one piece of placard I kept, and it stays because it is a law rather than a claim about you. What that decision cost him never showed up on any report he ever read. A reader who owns a business will run that arithmetic on their own business in about two seconds, and they will do it to themselves, which is the only way it holds.
  • "Basically a bomb in the kitchen" is yours, word for word. Do not soften it to unsafe equipment.
  • Permission: nobody named, no business named, and an OSHA call is a matter of record you were party to. This is the lowest risk of anything in here that has a villain in it. I would still keep the city and the year out of the caption.

The day the standard left

Post this third. Two posts in the same kitchen, one where an idea got built and one where a warning got overruled. This is the post where a reader finds out what happened to the building.

Format + dialStory post. Run hot for reach.
B-rollAn empty dining room with the lights up. A schedule on a wall. A back door. A parking lot at close. A set of keys going down on a counter.

Hooks text on screen

  • ContextI watched a profitable business get taken apart from the top, one decision at a time, and every one of those decisions was made to save money.
  • ContrarianBefore he stepped in, it was impossible to be bad at your job there. After, anybody could get one.
  • IntrigueHe got rid of the best manager he had. Then he did the thing that actually ended it. He took her job.

Caption

When I started there, it was impossible to be bad at your job.

Not because anyone was policing it. The standard was in the building. People who could not hold it felt it, and they opted out, or they got peer pressured out, and nobody above them ever had to do anything about it. It ran itself.

Then the owner got rid of the manager who built that, and stepped into the job himself.

What he brought with him was a vision he was going to implement regardless of what was best for the company, and a way of keeping everybody in a tiny box, because a tiny box is how you control things.

He stopped investing in good management. He hired people who did not care. He cut, and he saved, and he could show you exactly what he had saved.

Here is what he could not show you.

That place must have been enormously profitable before, because everybody in it was empowered and invested in the outcome, and every one of us worked like it was ours.

Afterward it was impossible to hire good people and impossible to keep the ones already there.

The best people left.

I was one of them.

DIGThe day you decided. Not the day you gave notice, the day you knew. Where you were, what had just happened in front of you, whether you told anybody that night. And one specific thing that got cut, because "he cut and he saved" is a summary and one cut is a scene.

Loop check

The cover carries a business, a risk and a slow clock. One question: what happens to the place. The head fake is that the ending is not a bankruptcy or a scandal, it is quiet, and it is one good employee walking out a door, which is considerably more frightening to an owner than a collapse. This one rehooks nowhere on purpose. It is meant to sit there.

Caption

I have helped build three businesses. All three of those founders sold.

It was life changing for them. It was not life changing for me.

Caption

I have been looking to buy a business.

Pulling demand out of thin air is not my strength. Distilling existing demand is not my strength either. I know that about myself and I have stopped pretending otherwise.

So I went looking, and I started talking to owners, and a lot of them do not have anything sellable yet. So I started helping them fix that.

What I left out on purpose

  • Every name. His, hers, the three founders, the theater, the restaurant, the agency. None of it is in this document and none of it is in my notes.
  • The word traumatic. You used it about a specific real person. It is the most accurate word you gave me all hour and it is the one word that turns a story into an accusation. It is not in any caption here.
  • The government, in anything commercial. It is a good story and it is in the holds. It is not in a post aimed at a business owner, for the reason under that hold.
