Storm's Story Bank

The new direction. Your core stories and content, built out into every format they can take, so you never run out and never start from a blank page. This is the sign language bank we pulled from today's session.

You teach hearing people how to communicate with the Deaf. Your people are the ones who cross paths with deaf folks and don't know what to do: the gas station attendant, the waiter, the teacher with one deaf student. You spent years as an interpreter and realized you don't love interpreting. You love watching two people connect with no one in the middle. So now you hand that connection over.
Every piece of content comes back to one thing: helping the Deaf community feel a sense of belonging. That's the anchor. If a post supports that, it belongs in the feed.

How to use this

Each panel below is one core story or content theme from your life and work. Open it and you'll find it told once in full, then a set of formats: a carousel, a belief line, a talking-head reel, a static image, a b-roll caption. Same idea, different shape, different job.

Talking-head is your superpower here. When you sign as you open the video, you get a visual hook, a verbal hook, and a text hook all at once. That's rare, and it's why this content can move fast.

This document grows. We add to it every session, and you keep it after we're done working together. It's yours.

Two conventions. Any line in a framed box is text that goes ON the video or image. The grey box beneath it is the caption you paste in. And any [SIGN: WORD] tag is a cue for you to demonstrate the actual sign. I'm not going to fake the mechanics of a sign; that's your expertise, and getting it wrong would be the fastest way to lose the room.

The four post types

Belonging is the thread under all of them, but each does a different job. Most of your content should live in the first two, because a hearing person does not wake up at 2am wondering how to talk to a deaf person. You have to show them why it matters before you ever teach them how.

Problem-awareness post.

Shows why this matters. What a deaf person actually experiences when no one around them can sign. This is most of your content, because the problem isn't obvious to a hearing person yet. Example: The Unavoidable Pity.

How-to post.

Teaches one real sign, given away free. No gatekeeping. They learn one thing from you and think, I could actually do this. Example: One Sign That Changes A Whole Interaction.

Story post.

A real moment from your years signing, told with an arc, ending on the moment so the point lands on its own. Example: He Doesn't Need Me Anymore.

Belief-shift post.

Plants a flag. A single stance about deafness, belonging, or connection that reveals who you are through what you refuse to accept. Example: Deaf Isn't A Tragedy.

Story 01 He Doesn't Need Me Anymore Story post · origin ▶

The day a kid walked up to ask you something, and the best thing you could do as an interpreter was point him somewhere else.

The story, once

You spent years as an interpreter. A hearing kid walks up to you mid-class and asks, "Can he play cards with us?" Nodding at the deaf student you're there for. And the move you're proudest of isn't interpreting the question. It's saying: don't ask me. Go ask him.

So the kid does. And the two of them figure it out, no interpreter, no middle-man, just two kids and a deck of cards. And you're standing off to the side, suddenly unnecessary, watching the thing you actually care about happen without you. That's when it clicked. You don't love interpreting. You love watching people connect directly. As the interpreter, you were always a little bit in the way of the thing you loved most.

Story 02 The Signing Santa Story post · belonging ▶

A little deaf boy who had seen a hundred Santas, and never once been able to tell one what he wanted.

The story, once

A second-grade boy, deaf, meets a Santa who signs. He's shy at first, hanging back. Then he catches it: Santa's hands are moving in his language. And you watch his whole face change. Not "a nice moment." A door opening. Because this kid has seen Santas his entire life, at the mall, in every movie, on every card, and every single one of them was a man he couldn't talk to.

Every hearing kid in that line got to climb up and tell Santa exactly what they wanted. This boy never had. Until a Santa took the time to learn his language and asked him, in signs, what he was hoping for. And in that one small moment he got to do the thing everyone else takes for granted. He got to belong.

Story 03 The Friend Who Didn't Make It Smaller Story post · anonymized ▶

The first time someone told you the real version of their life, in full, because you'd learned to meet them in their own language.

The story, once

One of your closest friends, who's deaf, started telling you about his childhood one day. The hard parts. The real parts. And here's what made it different: he wasn't code-switching. Deaf people usually soften their signing for a hearing person, slow it down, flatten it, make it easier to follow. He didn't. He signed it full speed, full depth, in his native language, the way he'd tell it to another deaf person.

And you sat there receiving a life story with nothing lost in the middle. Then you zoomed out and realized: I only get to be here for this because I learned the language. If you'd never taken sign, you'd have known this guy for years and only ever talked about the weather. Learning his language is the only reason you got to be a real friend instead of a polite one.

Theme 06 The Unavoidable Pity Problem-awareness ▶

The thing that makes a deaf person's day harder isn't the deafness. It's the face you make when you find out.

The idea, once

A deaf person walks into a restaurant and just wants to order a steak sandwich. They start signing, and the hearing person's face does the thing. Oh. Oh no. That flash of pity, the eyes going soft and sad, the whole energy shifting to "this poor person." Meanwhile the deaf person is thinking, I asked for a sandwich, why are you looking at me like my dog died.

Here's the reframe hearing people need: deafness is not a tragedy. They can't hear. That's it. The pity is the problem, not the deafness. And the fix is almost embarrassingly simple. If you could just sign "what can I get you," the whole thing turns from a sad little scene into a normal Tuesday.

The pity is the problem. And the fix is one sign: what can I get you.

Theme 07 Don't Make It A Big Deal Problem-awareness ▶

You think you're being accommodating. You're actually turning them into a spectacle.

The idea, once

A well-meaning hearing person notices someone is deaf and turns it into an event. Slows way down, over-gestures, gets loud, waves other staff over, announces it. They think they're being helpful. What they're actually doing is putting a spotlight on the one thing the deaf person didn't ask anyone to notice, and now the whole room is looking.

The most respectful thing you can do is treat them like a normal person having a normal interaction. Highlighting the deafness is what makes it isolating. Not the deafness itself.

Theme 08 You're Only Getting Half Problem-awareness · education ▶

There are two versions of sign language happening around you, and you've probably only ever seen the watered-down one.

The idea, once

When a deaf person signs to a hearing person, they usually code-switch: they slow down, simplify, and flatten it into something closer to English word order so we can follow. That's the version most hearing people have seen. Then there's native ASL, full speed, full grammar, full richness, the way deaf people sign to each other. It's a completely different, gorgeous language.

Most hearing people don't even know the second one exists. They think the slowed-down version is "sign language." It's like only ever hearing someone speak to you in short careful sentences and assuming that's all they've got. The real language, and the real person, is on the other side of learning it.

Format 1 · Talking-head reel
Show it. Sign a phrase slow/simplified, then sign it native speed. The visual proof is the post.
Spoken beats
There are two versions of sign language happening around you, and you've probably only ever seen the watered-down one. [Sign it native ASL.] This is the real language. Full speed, full grammar, the way deaf people actually talk to each other. Most hearing people don't even know it exists.

Theme 09 Language Deprivation Problem-awareness · education ▶

The quiet thing almost no hearing person knows: a lot of deaf kids grow up without full access to any language at all.

The idea, once

Here's something most hearing people have never heard of. For generations, schools have deprived deaf students of full language access. Not out of malice, usually, but a deaf kid without real communication at home or school can grow up without ever getting fluent in anything. No full language to think in, to argue in, to dream in. It has a name: language deprivation. And it's more common than anyone realizes.

The whole point of what you're building is the opposite of that. Full communication. Full access. A world where a deaf kid has more people who can actually talk to them, not fewer. That's what belonging means here.
