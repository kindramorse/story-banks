Text on the video
The case is often won before opening.
The caption

Most attorneys treat jury selection as a formality. I think it's one of the most important hours of the whole trial, and I've spent years refining how I do it.

The mistake is thinking your job is to talk the jury onto your side. It isn't. Your job is mostly to find the people who've already decided against you, and to do it in a way that lets them say so honestly.

One thing that works: instead of lecturing, I get the room building a list of what matters most to people. Family. Freedom. The ability to do your work. They come up with it, not me. Then, later, the case ties back to exactly those things.

Another is a scenario. I'll ask how they'd feel about a contractor who took a fifty-million-dollar loan and skipped the country, and whether the bank should get it back. Everyone agrees it should. Then I ask why a building can be worth fifty million but a person's ruined body somehow can't. You learn a lot from who bristles at that.

The craft is getting people to show you who they are before they're ever in the box.

If you're heading toward your first trials and jury selection is the part that scares you, that's a conversation I'm always up for. Send me a message.

One hard line here, and it's non-negotiable: method and principle only. Never a word about keeping or striking jurors by race, religion, or national origin. You said yourself that's illegal, so it never becomes content. The values board, the scenarios, reading a struggle versus an instant no. Nothing demographic, ever.

Early in my trial career, my instinct was to hammer every point until the jury couldn't miss it. I've learned that's one of the fastest ways to lose them.

Juries don't like being talked down to, and they don't like watching a lawyer beat up a witness for no reason. Give them the facts cleanly, respect their time, and get out of the way.

Here's the simplest system I run, and it's done more for client trust than any single result.

For years, I called clients when I had something to tell them. News came in, I'd call. That's what everyone does, and it's backwards.
